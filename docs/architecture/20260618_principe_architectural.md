# Author Discoverability Principle

## Context

The BHE-BE exploration revealed an unexpected pattern.

Many activities initially appeared difficult to represent in BHE.

However, repeated stress-tests showed that the core engine was often already capable of representing them.

The difficulty was not representation.

The difficulty was discovery.

Authors naturally followed the vocabulary and examples exposed by the system.

As a result, they were often guided toward evaluative interpretations of concepts that were actually more general.

---

## Principle

A capability that exists in the engine but remains difficult for authors to discover has limited practical value.

BHE should not only support pedagogical possibilities.

BHE should help authors recognize them.

---

## Consequences

When evaluating a concept, we should ask two different questions:

### Representation Question

Can the engine represent this?

### Discoverability Question

Would an author naturally realize that the engine can represent this?

These questions are independent.

A positive answer to the first does not imply a positive answer to the second.

---

## Example

A selection interaction may support:

* evaluation;
* diagnosis;
* reflection;
* survey;
* self-positioning.

The engine may already support all of these.

However, if the vocabulary, examples or documentation only suggest evaluation, authors may never discover the broader possibilities.

---

## Design Implication

Future improvements should not focus exclusively on adding representational power.

They should also improve:

* vocabulary;
* examples;
* documentation;
* author guidance;
* conceptual entry points.

---

## Lesson Learned

Not every difficulty encountered during authoring indicates a missing architectural concept.

Sometimes the architecture is sufficient.

What is missing is a path that helps authors see it.
