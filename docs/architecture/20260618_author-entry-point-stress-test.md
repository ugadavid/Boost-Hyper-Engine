# Author Entry Point Stress Test

## 1. Context

This document belongs to the experiment/bhe-be branch.

Recent work suggests:

~~~txt
The engine may already be able to represent
more than authors naturally discover.
~~~

The discoverability catalog connects pedagogical uses to interaction shapes. A prior authoring POC showed that BHE can represent a reflective activity, but that the author does not naturally find the path.

This stress test moves one step earlier:

~~~txt
Where does the author actually start?
~~~

Four hypotheses are considered:

- intention first;
- cognitive operation first;
- concrete content first;
- another entry point.

The goal is not to identify an ideal authoring method. It is to reconstruct the most plausible starting points visible in the Boost'English artifacts.

## 2. Methodological Limit

The corpus contains finished or near-finished pedagogical bricks, not recordings of authors thinking aloud.

Therefore this document cannot know:

- the first thought the teacher actually had;
- the chronological order of design decisions;
- which parts were inherited from a template;
- which ideas were added during revision;
- whether teacher extensions existed before the learner-facing core.

It can only inspect design traces:

- titles;
- stated goals;
- activity order;
- repeated templates;
- selected media;
- learner tasks;
- teacher notes;
- success conditions;
- unusual pedagogical moves.

Every reconstruction below should therefore be read as:

~~~txt
probable entry point suggested by the artifact
~~~

not:

~~~txt
verified author intention
~~~

## 3. Author Reconstruction

### Overview

| Brick or segment | Probable first design decision | Primary entry-point family | Secondary entry points | Confidence |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------- |
| Thinking in English | Help learners reduce translation and become more ready to speak | Learner problem / intended change | Affective orientation, reflection, speaking practice | Medium/high |
| Memorising Vocabulary | Help learners become more intentional about how they learn words | Pedagogical intention / learner strategy problem | Metacognition, concrete strategy content | High |
| Wayfinding | Create a credible task where learners ask for and give directions | Situated task / communicative success condition | Language content, map/resource, pair work | High |
| Passive Voice learner core | Teach and practise the passive voice | Content / syllabus topic | Recognition, transformation, song exposure | High |
| Passive Voice teacher extensions | Make passive voice useful for critical reading, explanation, and play | Classroom activity or functional use | Social mode, discourse intention, concrete objects | Medium |
| Countable / Uncountable - I learn | Help learners grasp the countable/uncountable distinction through examples | Content problem plus guided cognitive path | Compare, infer, classify, visual examples | Medium |
| Question Forms | Cover and practise question-form rules and cases | Content coverage / syllabus | Exercise sequence, correction, platform template | High |
| Conditionals | Teach conditional forms, then open imaginative use | Content first, generative situation later | Song/resource, production prompts | Medium/high |
| Present tense contrasts | Clarify a grammar contrast through contextual examples | Content distinction | Dialogue/resource, classification, personal production | High |
| Adjective/prefix bricks | Teach lexical or morphological patterns | Content/pattern | Matching gesture, visual or song resource | High |

The overview already weakens the idea of one universal entry point.

### Thinking In English

A plausible starting thought is:

~~~txt
Learners hesitate because they translate internally.
How can I help them notice this habit
and move toward more direct speech?
~~~

This brick does not look as though it began with:

~~~txt
I need a QCM.
~~~

The questionnaire, image prompts, pronunciation work, speaking task, and reflection all appear coordinated around a learner problem.

The probable primary entry point is neither content nor one cognitive operation. It is:

~~~txt
a desired learner change
+
an affective/metacognitive difficulty
~~~

The cognitive operations come afterward:

- notice;
- reflect;
- compare;
- produce;
- self-adjust.

The activity shapes then support that movement.

This is the strongest evidence for intention-first authoring in the corpus.

### Memorising Vocabulary

A plausible first thought is:

~~~txt
Learners need more deliberate ways
to learn and retain vocabulary.
~~~

The brick does not begin from one vocabulary list. Learning strategy is itself the subject.

The no-score questionnaire makes existing habits visible. Later activities expose and try alternatives, then return to reflection.

The probable entry point is:

~~~txt
learner strategy problem
-> metacognitive intention
~~~

Content is selected to support that intention.

The author may also have started from a familiar pedagogical question:

~~~txt
How do my learners currently memorize words?
~~~

That is more concrete than an abstract label such as Reflect, but still intention-led.

### Wayfinding

A plausible starting thought is:

~~~txt
Learners should be able to ask for
and give usable directions.
~~~

Yet the strongest organizing trace is not an abstract intention. It is a situation:

- one person needs to navigate;
- another gives directions;
- a map constrains the task;
- success can be tested by reaching a destination.

This suggests a task-first or situation-first entry point:

~~~txt
realistic communicative action
-> required language
-> supporting practice
~~~

Vocabulary, polite question forms, listening, maps, and peer work are selected because the task demands them.

Wayfinding therefore contradicts a simple intention-first model. The intention becomes visible through the concrete scenario.

### Passive Voice - Learner-Facing Core

A plausible starting thought is straightforward:

~~~txt
This brick must teach the passive voice.
~~~

The title, explanation, summary, exercises, and final check all point toward a content-first entry point.

The likely design path is:

~~~txt
grammar topic
-> forms and distinctions
-> examples
-> controlled practice
-> production
~~~

Cognitive operations such as identify, compare, and transform are important, but they look selected in response to the content.

This is strong evidence for content-first authoring.

### Passive Voice - Teacher Extensions

The teacher-facing extensions may have different entry points from the core brick.

The critical-reading extension may begin with:

~~~txt
How can passive voice reveal framing and agency?
~~~

The device explanation may begin with:

~~~txt
What real communicative use makes passive voice natural?
~~~

The guessing game may begin with:

~~~txt
What classroom game can make this form socially meaningful?
~~~

These are respectively:

- discourse intention first;
- functional situation first;
- classroom activity first.

One brick can therefore preserve traces of several authoring starts, perhaps from different design phases.

### Countable / Uncountable

The obvious content starting point is:

~~~txt
Learners need to distinguish countable
from uncountable nouns.
~~~

But the distinctive part of the brick is the reasoning tool:

~~~txt
Can I say 1 + 1 + 1 = many?
~~~

This suggests that the author did not stop at content coverage. A second design decision may have been:

~~~txt
How can the learner discover this distinction
through a concrete reasoning process?
~~~

The probable entry point is mixed:

- content distinction first;
- guided cognitive pathway immediately after.

This is not clean evidence for cognitive-operation-first authoring. It is evidence that cognition may become the organizing response to a content problem.

### Question Forms

The artifact appears closest to:

~~~txt
Learners need coverage and practice
of question forms and related cases.
~~~

The repeated rule, exercise, and additional-rule structure suggests:

- syllabus/content first;
- coverage constraint;
- exercise accumulation;
- platform sequence template.

This is the strongest content-first case.

It also shows that authors may start from obligation:

~~~txt
I have to cover this domain.
~~~

That is not exactly content preference or pedagogical intention. It is a curriculum constraint.

### Other Grammar Bricks

The corpus cartography reinforces the pattern.

Bricks titled around:

- conditionals;
- adjective prefixes and suffixes;
- comparatives and superlatives;
- present and past tense contrasts;
- adjective/adverb distinctions;

strongly suggest content-first authoring.

Many then add:

- a song;
- a dialogue;
- images;
- a matching task;
- voicework;
- a personal speaking prompt.

Those later choices may enrich pedagogy substantially. But the visible entry point remains the language item to teach.

The corpus as a whole therefore contains more content-first traces than the high-potential POCs alone might suggest.

## 4. Entry Point Families

The four initial hypotheses are not sufficient by themselves.

Several additional entry points appear repeatedly.

### Content First

Typical first thought:

~~~txt
I need to teach this grammar point,
vocabulary domain, or language contrast.
~~~

Strong examples:

- Passive Voice;
- Question Forms;
- tense contrasts;
- adjective and prefix bricks;
- comparatives and superlatives.

This is the most frequent visible entry point across the corpus.

### Intention Or Learner-Change First

Typical first thought:

~~~txt
I want learners to become more aware,
more deliberate, or more ready to act.
~~~

Strong examples:

- Thinking in English;
- Memorising Vocabulary.

This entry point creates some of the richest BHE-BE signals, but it is not the most frequent.

### Cognitive Problem First

Typical first thought:

~~~txt
Learners need to notice,
compare, infer, or distinguish something.
~~~

Countable / Uncountable comes closest.

However, cognitive operations rarely appear as isolated starting labels. They appear embedded in concrete teaching questions:

~~~txt
How can learners see the difference?
How can they infer the rule?
~~~

The corpus gives limited evidence that teachers begin with formal cognitive-operation vocabulary.

### Situated Task Or Success Condition First

Typical first thought:

~~~txt
Learners should be able to accomplish this action.
~~~

Wayfinding is the strongest example.

The task determines content, interaction, social mode, and success criterion.

This is a distinct entry point that the original A/B/C hypotheses do not capture well.

### Learner Difficulty First

Typical first thought:

~~~txt
Learners struggle with this habit,
fear, confusion, or recurring error.
~~~

Thinking in English and Memorising Vocabulary both contain this signal.

The desired intention emerges as a response to the difficulty.

### Resource Or Stimulus First

Some bricks begin with:

- a song;
- a video;
- a dialogue;
- an image;
- a map;
- an article.

The resource may be selected after the goal, but sometimes it appears generative:

~~~txt
This song contains useful examples.
What can learners notice through it?
~~~

The finished PDFs cannot reveal the direction of causality. Still, resource-first authoring is plausible and should not be ignored.

### Classroom Activity First

Teacher notes frequently suggest:

- guessing games;
- peer exchanges;
- map challenges;
- cards;
- group discussion;
- story prompts.

The starting thought may be:

~~~txt
I know a classroom activity that works.
Which content can it carry?
~~~

This is especially visible in teacher-facing extensions.

### Constraint Or Template First

Boost'English bricks often follow recognizable phases such as:

- I start;
- I learn;
- I practise;
- I say/share;
- test or reflection.

Authors may therefore begin from an empty slot in a template:

~~~txt
I need an I start activity for this brick.
~~~

Curriculum coverage, platform affordances, available media, time, copyright, and classroom resources may also shape the first decision.

This point matters because real authoring does not begin in a conceptual vacuum.

## 5. Recurrent Patterns

### Grammar Bricks Tend To Start From Content

The strongest corpus-wide regularity is:

~~~txt
grammar or lexical topic
-> examples and explanation
-> interaction choice
-> practice
-> later production or extension
~~~

This does not mean the pedagogy is shallow. Passive Voice and Countable / Uncountable show that content-first bricks can develop rich intentions and cognitive paths.

### Metacognitive Bricks Tend To Start From A Learner Problem

Thinking in English and Memorising Vocabulary appear closer to:

~~~txt
learner habit or difficulty
-> awareness intention
-> reflective interaction
-> later strategy or production
~~~

Here the interaction shape is clearly downstream.

### Situated Communication Tends To Start From A Task

Wayfinding appears closer to:

~~~txt
credible action
-> success condition
-> language resources
-> practice and social verification
~~~

The practical scenario organizes the brick more strongly than one abstract cognitive operation.

### Teacher Extensions Often Start From Activity Or Mediation

The For teachers material frequently begins from:

- a discussion;
- a game;
- pair work;
- a physical resource;
- a real text;
- a classroom variation.

These entry points may be absent from the learner-facing core but pedagogically important.

### Cognitive Operations Usually Clarify Rather Than Initiate

Notice, compare, infer, classify, transform, and produce are highly useful for describing what the learner does.

But the artifacts rarely suggest that an author began with:

~~~txt
I need an infer activity.
~~~

More often the reasoning is:

~~~txt
I need to teach this distinction.
How can learners infer it?
~~~

Cognitive operations appear to be strong design lenses, but not the dominant observable starting point.

## 6. Contradictory Evidence

### A Content Title Does Not Prove A Content-First Process

Passive Voice may be titled after its final curriculum topic even if the author began from a song, article, or communicative idea.

The artifact shows hierarchy, not chronology.

### An Intention-Rich Sequence May Have Been Built Backward

Thinking in English looks intention-led, but it may have started from existing videos, prompts, or a required speaking outcome and only later gained a coherent reflective arc.

### A Resource May Be Entry Point Or Decoration

Songs appear repeatedly. They may inspire the brick, provide authentic examples, or simply satisfy a recurring format.

The PDF cannot tell us which.

### Teacher Extensions May Be Original Or Retrofitted

The richest Passive Voice ideas live in For teachers. They may reveal the author's original pedagogical imagination or later enrichment around a conventional grammar core.

### One Brick May Have Multiple Authors Or Design Passes

A core exercise, revision, teacher note, and platform adaptation may each begin from different points.

Reconstructing one unified author mind may be false.

### The Selected POCs Are Biased

Thinking in English, Memorising Vocabulary, Wayfinding, and Countable / Uncountable were selected partly because they contain unusual signals.

They overrepresent intention-first, task-first, and guided-discovery authoring.

The wider corpus contains many more grammar-topic titles and content-first sequences.

This correction is essential.

## 7. Consequences For Author Discoverability

### No single natural doorway

The evidence does not support one mandatory authoring start.

Different authors and activities appear to begin from:

- content;
- learner difficulty;
- desired change;
- real-world task;
- resource;
- classroom activity;
- curriculum or platform constraint.

BHE should not assume that every author arrives saying:

~~~txt
My pedagogical intention is Reflect.
~~~

Nor should it assume:

~~~txt
My cognitive operation is Compare.
~~~

Many authors will arrive with:

~~~txt
I need to teach the passive voice.
I have this dialogue.
My learners translate before speaking.
I want them to give usable directions.
I need an I start activity.
~~~

All are credible starts.

### The most frequent observed start

Across the corpus, the most frequent visible entry point is:

~~~txt
concrete content or curriculum topic
~~~

This is especially clear in the grammar bricks.

But frequency is not the same as pedagogical superiority. The most distinctive bricks often begin from a learner problem, intended change, or situated task.

### The most useful bridge

The authoring challenge is not to replace content-first thinking with intention-first thinking.

The useful movement is:

~~~txt
start where the author is
then make adjacent dimensions visible
~~~

For a content-first author:

~~~txt
What should learners do with this content?
What should change after the activity?
~~~

For an intention-first author:

~~~txt
What learner response would provide evidence?
Which content and interaction support that intention?
~~~

For a task-first author:

~~~txt
What makes the action successful?
Which cognitive operations and language resources are required?
~~~

For a resource-first author:

~~~txt
Why is this resource pedagogically useful?
What should learners notice, infer, practise, or produce?
~~~

This is an implication for discoverability, not an implementation proposal.

## 8. Recommendation

Conclusion:

~~~txt
Different authors appear to start from different places.
~~~

More precisely:

- content is the most frequent visible entry point across the corpus;
- intention or learner change is strongest in metacognitive bricks;
- situated task is strongest in action-oriented communication;
- cognitive operation is rarely the first visible decision, but often the best clarifying lens after the start;
- resource, classroom activity, template, and constraint are credible additional entry points.

If BHE helps an author begin, the most natural approach is not one abstract gateway.

The main implication is:

~~~txt
author discoverability should be multi-entry
and progressively clarifying
~~~

An author should be able to begin with:

- what they need to teach;
- what learners struggle with;
- what learners should become able to do;
- a concrete question or resource;
- a classroom activity;
- a cognitive operation;
- a pedagogical intention.

Then BHE can help reveal the dimensions not yet considered.

This does not stabilize an authoring model. It preserves an observation:

~~~txt
the starting point varies;
the need for clarification recurs
~~~

## 9. Final Findings

### Main conclusion

Different authors and different pedagogical families appear to start from different places.

### Most frequent observed entry point

Concrete content or curriculum topic.

### Strong counterexamples

- Thinking in English: learner difficulty and desired posture change;
- Memorising Vocabulary: learning-strategy intention;
- Wayfinding: situated task and real-world success condition;
- teacher extensions: classroom activity, resource, or social organization.

### Main implication for BHE

Do not make authors translate their first thought into one privileged abstract vocabulary before they can begin.

The engine's hidden capabilities will become easier to discover if different starting points can lead toward the same clarifying questions:

- learner cognition;
- pedagogical purpose;
- response semantics;
- interaction shape;
- evaluation policy;
- meaningful result.
